# High-Temperature Chemical Compatibility Among Refractory Crucible and Susceptor Materials (1700–2500 °C)

## 1. Introduction

High-temperature processing of advanced materials — including sublimation growth of AlN and SiC single crystals, sintering of ultra-high-temperature ceramics, and zone refining of refractory metals — routinely employs crucibles, susceptors, and containment made from tantalum, graphite/carbon, boron nitride (BN), magnesia (MgO), alumina (Al₂O₃), and zirconia (ZrO₂). At temperatures of 1700–2500 °C, none of these materials can be regarded as universally inert; pairwise chemical reactions including carburization, carbothermal reduction, eutectic liquid formation, oxygen gettering, and vapor-phase transport can compromise crucible integrity, contaminate growing crystals, and alter the local thermochemical environment. The following sections review the most important compatibility issues.

## 2. Carburization of Tantalum: TaC and Ta₂C Formation

When tantalum is exposed to carbon or graphite at temperatures in the 1700–2500 °C range, reactive diffusion leads to the sequential formation of Ta₂C and TaC phases (avdeev2012developmentof2" pages 25-28). This process is so rapid at these temperatures that practical crystal growth systems deliberately pre-carbonize tantalum crucibles before use: the resulting TaC–Ta₂C ceramic coating is thermally and chemically stable, capable of enduring over 3000 hours of cumulative service in graphite-heated furnaces at 1800–2000 °C (avdeev2012developmentof2" pages 25-28). In AlN sublimation growth, for example, bulk crystal growth is performed at 1800–1900 °C in TaC crucibles under nitrogen atmosphere, and the protective TaC layer is converted into TaCN during the growth process (sumathi2021commonissuesin pages 2-4). The pre-carbonization coating also prevents the formation of low-melting-temperature eutectics between bare tantalum and silicon or aluminum species (Ta–Si, Ta–Al), which would otherwise be highly detrimental (avdeev2012developmentof2" pages 25-28, sumathi2021commonissuesin pages 5-7). However, even with TaC-coated crucibles in a graphite heater environment, carbon diffusion into the growing crystal remains a persistent source of contamination, with EPMA measurements showing carbon concentrations of several atomic percent in crystals grown in such setups (sumathi2021commonissuesin pages 5-7).

## 3. Alumina–Carbon Reactions and Eutectics Near 1800 °C

Alumina is not inert in contact with carbon or carbon-bearing atmospheres at temperatures above approximately 1800 °C. Thermodynamic analysis of the SiC–Al₂O₃ system by Baud et al. shows that the principal high-temperature vaporization reaction is 2 SiC(s) + Al₂O₃(s) + Al₂O(g) → 2 SiO(g) + 2 CO(g) + 4 Al(g), and that the vaporization is congruent between 1200 and 2170 K but becomes non-congruent above 2170 K when liquid silicon forms (baud2003hightemperaturesintering pages 1-2, baud2003hightemperaturesintering pages 5-7). In the presence of excess carbon, the alumina–aluminum carbide (Al₂O₃–Al₄C₃) system exhibits eutectic liquid formation between 2220 and 2273 K (~1947–2000 °C), and a ternary Al₂O₃–Al₄C₃–SiC liquid phase is also possible (baud2003hightemperaturesintering pages 1-2). These reactions mean that alumina crucibles or insulation in contact with graphite susceptors or carbon-containing atmospheres may suffer progressive degradation, weight loss via volatile Al₂O(g) and CO(g) transport, and even liquid formation at temperatures approaching 2000 °C. Vapor transport of Al₂O(g) and Al(g) can also lead to contamination of nearby specimens or growing crystals (baud2003hightemperaturesintering pages 2-3).

## 4. Carbothermal Reduction and Consumption of Zirconia by Carbon

Zirconia is consumed by carbon through the well-characterized carbothermal reduction reaction: 3C(s) + ZrO₂(s) = ZrC(s) + 2CO(g) (sacks2004carbothermalreductionsynthesis pages 2-3). Using solution-derived precursors that achieve nanometer-level mixing, Sacks et al. demonstrated that ZrC formation becomes evident at temperatures as low as 1200 °C and the reaction is substantially complete by 1400–1500 °C (sacks2004carbothermalreductionsynthesis pages 10-10, sacks2004carbothermalreductionsynthesis pages 1-2). The process proceeds through an intermediate zirconium oxycarbide phase, ZrO_yC_(1+y−x), with the generalized reaction ZrO_yC_(1+y−x)(s) + xC(s) = ZrC(s) + yCO(g) (sacks2004carbothermalreductionsynthesis pages 5-7). Oxygen content in the ZrC product decreases progressively with temperature; at 1800 °C, nearly stoichiometric ZrC with only ~0.1 wt% oxygen is obtained (sacks2004carbothermalreductionsynthesis pages 3-5). The crystallite growth follows a "contracting volume" model where the ZrC shell grows inward from the surface of ZrO₂ particles (sacks2004carbothermalreductionsynthesis pages 5-7). These findings demonstrate that zirconia crucibles or insulation in direct contact with graphite or carbon-rich environments at temperatures above ~1200 °C will be progressively consumed, making ZrO₂ a poor choice for long-duration containment against carbon at the temperatures of interest.

## 5. Tantalum–Boron Nitride Interface Degradation

Boron nitride is commonly employed in high-temperature furnace assemblies as a separator, insulator, or structural element alongside tantalum crucibles. While BN possesses excellent thermal shock resistance and chemical inertness in many environments, direct prolonged contact between Ta and BN at temperatures above ~1700 °C can lead to interfacial reaction. In AlN crystal growth systems, the combination of TaC crucibles with graphite and nitrogen environments has been extensively studied, and the general principle that refractory metals react with BN to form borides and/or nitrides at extreme temperatures is well established (avdeev2012developmentof2" pages 5-7, sumathi2021commonissuesin pages 2-4). For practical crystal growth, tungsten crucibles are avoided with SiC seeds because Si in SiC reacts with W to form low-temperature tungsten silicide eutectics; instead, TaC crucibles are preferred (sumathi2021commonissuesin pages 5-7). The Ta–BN interface should be treated as metastable at temperatures above 1700 °C, and direct long-duration contact should be minimized where contamination or interfacial embrittlement is a concern.

## 6. Tantalum–MgO Interface Degradation

The strong oxygen affinity of tantalum, with a binding energy of Q_O = 166–179 kcal/g-atom (glebovsky2015physicalandtechnological pages 10-13), means that tantalum in contact with oxide ceramics such as MgO can abstract oxygen from the ceramic at high temperatures. This thermodynamic driving force for reduction of MgO at the Ta–MgO interface is expected to lead to progressive degradation, particularly under reducing conditions or in vacuum at temperatures above 1700 °C. The Inouye review of refractory metal–gas interactions confirms that tantalum alloys containing reactive alloying elements (Ti, Zr, Hf) show enhanced oxygen uptake compared to unalloyed metals (inouye1968interactionsofrefractory pages 26-30), and the same oxygen gettering tendency would apply at metal–oxide interfaces. While MgO has one of the highest melting points among oxide ceramics (~2852 °C), its stability against reduction by tantalum at the interface limits its reliability as a crucible material in direct contact with tantalum hardware.

## 7. Tantalum Vapor Transport and Deposition on Ceramic Specimens

At temperatures from approximately 1200 °C upward in vacuum environments, tantalum reacts with residual oxygen to form volatile oxide species, principally TaO(g) and TaO₂(g) (inouye1968interactionsofrefractory pages 30-35). Unlike tungsten and molybdenum, which show constant sublimation rates at given temperature and pressure, the sublimation rate of tantalum varies markedly with time due to the competition between oxygen dissolution into the metal bulk and volatile oxide formation at the surface (inouye1968interactionsofrefractory pages 35-38). As oxygen progressively saturates the near-surface region, the volatile oxide generation rate increases until reaching a plateau. These volatile tantalum oxide species can be transported through the gas phase and deposited on cooler surfaces, contaminating nearby ceramic specimens or growing crystals. The pressures of volatile tantalum oxides as a function of temperature have been characterized at oxygen pressures of ~1.7 × 10⁻⁷ torr, with the generation rates passing through characteristic maxima that are temperature-dependent (inouye1968interactionsofrefractory pages 30-35).

## 8. Oxygen Gettering by Tantalum

Tantalum is one of the strongest oxygen getters among the refractory metals. In the solid state, tantalum desorbs oxygen primarily as monoxide (TaO) and dioxide (TaO₂) species, with an evaporation ratio of 28–42, indicating that oxygen can be removed by high-temperature vacuum annealing (glebovsky2015physicalandtechnological pages 10-13). Above the melting point of tantalum, the evaporation ratio increases dramatically to 2000–3000, reflecting intensive oxygen removal from the molten state (glebovsky2015physicalandtechnological pages 10-13). This oxygen gettering capability has dual practical implications: on one hand, tantalum components in a furnace can serve as sacrificial getters that lower the oxygen partial pressure and protect oxygen-sensitive materials; on the other hand, oxygen abstracted from nearby oxide ceramics (MgO, Al₂O₃, ZrO₂) can destabilize those ceramics and alter the local chemical environment. For AlN crystal growth, the presence of oxygen is particularly detrimental due to the formation of oxynitrides and stacking faults that decrease thermal conductivity and introduce sub-band-gap optical absorption (avdeev2012developmentof2" pages 25-28). Hence, tantalum's gettering effect must be carefully managed in systems where both tantalum components and oxide ceramics are present.

## 9. Practical Implications and Material Selection

The two-stage crucible technology developed for AlN sublimation growth illustrates how these compatibility constraints are managed in practice: initial seeding and growth on SiC substrates is performed at 1800–1900 °C in pre-carbonized TaC crucibles within graphite RF-heated furnaces, while subsequent bulk crystal growth at 2050–2250 °C uses tungsten crucibles in tungsten resistively heated furnaces to eliminate carbon contamination (avdeev2012developmentof2" pages 25-28, avdeev2012developmentof2" pages 28-35). The pre-carbonized TaC–Ta₂C coating on tantalum crucibles endures cumulative service exceeding 3000 hours, preventing the formation of detrimental Ta–Si and Ta–Al eutectics (avdeev2012developmentof2" pages 25-28). Carbon contamination from the graphite heater environment remains a persistent issue, with Si and C impurities reaching several atomic percent in AlN crystals grown in graphite-equipped systems (sumathi2021commonissuesin pages 5-7, sumathi2021commonissuesin pages 9-11).
