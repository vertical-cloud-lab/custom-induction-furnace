## Section A: Specific findings

1. **Quote:** “We are pleased to submit our manuscript”  
   **Problem:** Stock throat-clearing. It delays the action without adding content.  
   **Replacement:** “We submit our manuscript”

2. **Quote:** “Two results should be of particular interest to your readers.”  
   **Problem:** The paragraph transition is logical, but “should be of particular interest” tells the editor how to react and sounds promotional.  
   **Replacement:** “The manuscript reports two results that demonstrate the instrument’s performance.”

3. **Quote:** “First, nickel annealed in this furnace produced high-quality electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD) patterns with no specimen preparation at all: samples went straight from the furnace chamber into the electron microscope, with no grinding, polishing, or etching.”  
   **Problem:** “At all” and “went straight” are conversational intensifiers; “samples” shifts terminology from “specimen.” The sentence also repeats the no-preparation claim before defining it.  
   **Replacement:** “First, nickel annealed in the furnace yielded high-quality electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD) patterns without grinding, polishing, or etching; the specimens were transferred directly from the furnace to the scanning electron microscope.”

4. **Quote:** “We attribute this to the purity of the annealing environment.”  
   **Problem:** This is stronger and narrower than the manuscript. The paper says “we surmise” and proposes several contributors: removal of the deformed near-surface layer during annealing, low oxygen potential, and surface diffusion (manuscript lines 203–216). “Purity” is also vague.  
   **Replacement:** “The manuscript discusses how the near-solidus anneal, low-oxygen environment, and surface diffusion may contribute to this result.”

5. **Quote:** “Second, a small change to the sample assembly extends the same furnace to ceramics, which do not couple to the induction field.”  
   **Problem:** The sentence does two jobs: introduces the second result and explains induction coupling. “A small change” underspecifies that the materials in contact with the specimen were changed. Not all ceramics necessarily fail to couple, so the unqualified clause is too broad.  
   **Replacement:** “Second, changing the materials in the sample assembly extended the furnace to yttria-stabilized zirconia (YSZ), which does not couple directly to the radiofrequency field.”

6. **Quote:** “It coarsened yttria-stabilized zirconia grains in 45 minutes at 2500 °C — a treatment that took 228 hours at 1600 °C in a conventional box furnace.”  
   **Problem:** The manuscript does not report the identical treatment at both conditions. It reports comparable but not identical coarsening: approximately 20–90 µm in the induction furnace versus approximately 10–80 µm in the box furnace (lines 281–285). The current wording implies a controlled head-to-head comparison and identical starting material.  
   **Replacement:** “The system coarsened YSZ grains from approximately 20 to 90 µm in 45 min at 2500 °C, compared with approximately 10 to 80 µm after 228 h at 1600 °C in a conventional box furnace.”

7. **Quote:** “The instrument behind these results is a fully documented, open retrofit.”  
   **Problem:** The transition from results to instrument is good, but “fully” is an empty intensifier and “open retrofit” may make an editor pause.  
   **Replacement:** “These results were obtained with the open, documented retrofit described in the manuscript.”

8. **Quote:** “Annealing a metal near its melting point without oxidizing it normally requires an expensive turn-key vacuum induction furnace.”  
   **Problem:** “Normally requires” is too absolute. The manuscript says commercial vacuum induction furnaces meeting the stated requirements cost $50,000–$200,000 or more, while resistive furnaces have lower temperatures and slower ramps (lines 34–57). Use “turnkey” without a hyphen.  
   **Replacement:** “Commercial turnkey vacuum induction furnaces capable of near-melting-point annealing under controlled atmospheres typically cost $50,000–$200,000 or more.”

9. **Quote:** “Many laboratories own, or can affordably buy, a bare commercial radiofrequency induction generator; what they lack is the computer control, vacuum and gas handling, and optical temperature feedback that turn it into a scientific instrument.”  
   **Problem:** “Many laboratories own” and “can affordably buy” are unsupported in the manuscript. “What they lack” makes a broad claim about laboratories, and “turn it into a scientific instrument” is marketing language.  
   **Replacement:** “Bare commercial radiofrequency induction generators are widely available but lack the computer control, vacuum and gas handling, and optical temperature feedback needed for this application.”

10. **Quote:** “Our manuscript supplies that missing layer.”  
    **Problem:** Restates the preceding sentence and sounds promotional.  
    **Replacement:** **Cut.**

11. **Quote:** “Because it needs only the generator's analog power-control input, the retrofit is independent of the generator's make and model”  
    **Problem:** The manuscript requires a **monotonic** analog power-control input and says users must redetermine system-specific parameters after transfer (lines 141–142, 302–305). “Independent” overstates interchangeability.  
    **Replacement:** “Because the retrofit requires only a monotonic analog power-control input, it can be transferred between generator makes and models, with recalibration for each configuration.”

12. **Quote:** “the reference build, on a 6 kW solid-state generator, brings the entire system to about $38k.”  
    **Problem:** “Brings … to” is indirect, “entire” is unnecessary, and “$38k” is less formal than “$38,000.”  
    **Replacement:** “The reference build uses a 6 kW solid-state generator and costs approximately $38,000.”

13. **Quote:** “The manuscript validates the instrument quantitatively: a power–temperature calibration linear to R² = 0.991 over 1200–1400 °C, eight independent 12-hour nickel anneals that reproduced their soak temperature to 1201.2 ± 1.3 °C (a variation of 0.11%), and stable closed-loop soaks up to 40 hours.”  
    **Problem:** This dense sentence asks the reader to absorb three different validation results at once. “Linear” needs the manuscript’s fixed-configuration qualification; “soak,” “variation,” and “closed-loop” are avoidable jargon. The paper calls 0.11% a coefficient of variation.  
    **Replacement:** “For a fixed configuration, the power–temperature calibration was approximately linear from 1200 to 1400 °C ($R^2=0.991$). Across eight separate 12 h nickel anneals, the mean hold temperature was 1201.2 ± 1.3 °C, corresponding to a 0.11% coefficient of variation; feedback-controlled holds remained stable for as long as 40 h.”

14. **Quote:** “The work is fully reproducible.”  
    **Problem:** Reproducibility has not been independently demonstrated by another laboratory. The files support reproducibility, but “fully reproducible” overclaims.  
    **Replacement:** “The manuscript provides the files and data needed to reproduce the system and evaluate the reported runs.”

15. **Quote:** “Complete design files, an itemized bill of materials, the LabVIEW control software, and all underlying run data are openly available on GitHub”  
    **Problem:** “Complete” and “all” are absolute. The manuscript states that design files, the bill of materials, software, and data supporting the findings are available; it specifically mentions raw logs for reported specimens rather than every run ever performed (lines 198–203, 421–431).  
    **Replacement:** “Design files, an itemized bill of materials, LabVIEW control software, and data supporting the findings are available on GitHub”

16. **Quote:** “The supplementary material links the reported specimens to their raw furnace run logs.”  
    **Problem:** Accurate, but it can be joined to the preceding data-availability sentence to improve flow and reduce paragraph fragmentation.  
    **Replacement:** “The supplementary material links each reported specimen to its raw furnace log.”

    **Problem:** This is an internal drafting instruction, not submission-ready prose. A preprint disclosure is acceptable but unnecessary if no preprint exists.

18. **Quote:** “[Related work by the authors, per the journal's cover-letter guidance — list any prior publications by the author group on grain growth, grain-boundary characterization, or instrument development, e.g., publications by O. Johnson's group that motivated the furnace.]”  
    **Problem:** This is another internal instruction and would be an abrupt jump if left unresolved. More importantly, the current AIP author-instruction page gives the “list related work” cover-letter guidance under *Applied Physics Letters*, not under the journal-specific *Review of Scientific Instruments* instructions. It is therefore not an apparent RSI requirement.  
    **Replacement:** Add only directly related author publications that help the editor assess novelty; otherwise **cut** the placeholder. It remains unchanged in Section B as requested. ## Section B: Revised letter body

Dear Editor,

We submit our manuscript, "Retrofitting a commercial RF induction generator into a computer-controlled, vacuum and gas integrated annealing system for reactive-metal grain growth," for consideration as a regular contributed article in *Review of Scientific Instruments*.

The manuscript reports two results that demonstrate the instrument’s performance. First, nickel annealed in the furnace yielded high-quality electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD) patterns without grinding, polishing, or etching; the specimens were transferred directly from the furnace to the scanning electron microscope. The manuscript discusses how the near-solidus anneal, low-oxygen environment, and surface diffusion may contribute to this result. Second, changing the materials in the sample assembly extended the furnace to yttria-stabilized zirconia (YSZ), which does not couple directly to the radiofrequency field. The system coarsened YSZ grains from approximately 20 to 90 µm in 45 min at 2500 °C, compared with approximately 10 to 80 µm after 228 h at 1600 °C in a conventional box furnace.

These results were obtained with the open, documented retrofit described in the manuscript. Commercial turnkey vacuum induction furnaces capable of near-melting-point annealing under controlled atmospheres typically cost $50,000–$200,000 or more. Bare commercial radiofrequency induction generators are widely available but lack the computer control, vacuum and gas handling, and optical temperature feedback needed for this application. The retrofit adds these components. Because it requires only a monotonic analog power-control input, it can be transferred between generator makes and models, with recalibration for each configuration. The reference build uses a 6 kW solid-state generator and costs approximately $38,000.

The manuscript validates the instrument quantitatively. For a fixed configuration, the power–temperature calibration was approximately linear from 1200 to 1400 °C ($R^2=0.991$). Across eight separate 12 h nickel anneals, the mean hold temperature was 1201.2 ± 1.3 °C, corresponding to a 0.11% coefficient of variation; feedback-controlled holds remained stable for as long as 40 h.

The manuscript provides the files and data needed to reproduce the system and evaluate the reported runs. Design files, an itemized bill of materials, LabVIEW control software, and data supporting the findings are available on GitHub (https://github.com/vertical-cloud-lab/custom-induction-furnace), with an archival copy deposited at Zenodo (https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.20878017). The supplementary material links each reported specimen to its raw furnace log.
