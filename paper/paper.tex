\begin{abstract}
High-temperature vacuum annealing near a metal's melting point drives
controlled grain growth but normally requires an expensive turn-key vacuum
induction furnace. We describe an open, generator-agnostic modernization stack
that converts a bare commercial RF induction generator into a
computer-controlled, vacuum-integrated, pyrometer-feedback annealing system:
LabVIEW/DAQ analog power control, dual-wavelength optical temperature
feedback, a high-vacuum quartz-tube chamber, and an in-house machined graphite
crucible/susceptor for metal specimens. The layer transfers between generators
that expose a monotonic analog power-control input; the reproducible build is
anchored on a current 6\,kW solid-state generator. In operation the system
shows a fixed-geometry power--temperature calibration ($R^2 = 0.991$ over
1200--1400\,\celsius), repeatable 1200\,\celsius{} / 12\,h nickel anneals
($1201.2 \pm 1.3$\,\celsius{} across 8 runs), stable soaks up to 40\,h at
1325\,\celsius, and annealed-nickel microstructures characterized by optical
metallography, SEM, and EBSD. A tantalum-susceptor variant extends the
furnace to non-coupling ceramics at 2000--2350\,\celsius, demonstrated with
YSZ grain growth. Complete design files, bill of materials, control software,
and data are openly available.
\end{abstract}

\keywords{induction furnace; grain growth; vacuum annealing; LabVIEW;
pyrometer; open-source hardware}

%==============================================================
\section{Introduction}
%==============================================================

High-temperature annealing near a metal's melting point drives controlled
grain growth, central to studying microstructure--property relationships in
metals such as nickel and
iron~\cite{liu2021studyofgrain,mohrbacher2026applicationofmicroalloying,alogab2007theinfluenceof,tian2009experimentalandsimulation,moore2017graincoarseningbehaviour}.
Doing this without oxidizing the sample requires both high temperature
($\sim$1400--1500\,\celsius) and high vacuum or an inert
atmosphere~\cite{zhornyak1982reductionanddecarburization,muramatsu2005gascontaminationdue}.
Commercial vacuum induction furnaces meeting these requirements are closed,
turn-key systems costing \$50k--\$200k+; resistive tube and box furnaces reach
lower temperatures with slower ramps and offer no susceptor route to
non-coupling charges; and bare industrial induction generators are widely
available but ship without vacuum, optical temperature feedback, or computer
setpoint control.

This work adds exactly that missing layer: a bare RF induction
generator~\cite{rudnev2017handbookofinduction,lucia2014inductionheatingtechnology}
is retrofitted into a full annealing system with computer power control,
closed-loop optical temperature feedback, a high-vacuum quartz-tube chamber,
and a machined graphite crucible/susceptor. The stack is deliberately
generator-agnostic: the only requirement is an analog power-control input
(e.g. 4--20\,mA or 0--5\,V) with a monotonic command-to-power
response, since the pyrometer feedback and PID calibration absorb any
nonlinearity. The generator internals are not reproduced; a compatible
generator needs to be supplied. The graphite
crucible, which couples strongly to the RF field, serves the metal
grain-growth charges; for non-coupling ceramics such as YSZ, an entirely
separate tantalum-susceptor charge stack replaces it (Sec.~\ref{sec:ysz}).

The reproducible build documented here is anchored on a current, USA-sourced
6\,kW solid-state generator whose controller accepts a standard 4--20\,mA
power setpoint. The generator, heating head, controller, chiller package
was quoted at $\approx$\$16.5k, with the retrofit components itemized in the
supplementary bill of materials. This is a small fraction of turn-key cost,
in a form that is repairable and modifiable by the user.

\section{System design and description}
%==============================================================

The system comprises five subsystems (Fig.~\ref{fig:overview});
Fig.~\ref{fig:furnacephoto} shows the assembled system at operating power.

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.76\linewidth]{fig_system_overview_v2.png}
\caption{System overview. The generator drives the water-cooled heating
head/work coil around the vertically suspended quartz-tube chamber; the
chamber stack is joined by KF40 flanges to the ceiling-suspended pyrometer
housing, and an overpressure relief valve and flexible bellows lead past the
pressure sensor to the turbo pumping station.}
\label{fig:overview}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.4\linewidth]{fig_furnace_photo.jpg}
\caption{The assembled, functioning furnace with the main components labeled;
the quartz chamber glows at the sample-stack position, the pyrometer sights
down the chamber axis, the vent valve admits the argon backfill, and the
gauge, turbopump, and separately supported roughing pump complete the vacuum
path.}
\label{fig:furnacephoto}
\end{figure}

The heating subsystem is a 6\,kW solid-state induction generator driving a
water-cooled copper work coil
($\sim$3\,in tall, 2.5\,in ID, $\sim$6.5 turns, tuned to the sample/crucible
stack~\cite{vankan2024electromagneticinductionheating,xu2026designandoptimization});
the coil conductors carry both the RF current and the coolant, and cooling
water flows in series from the chiller through the generator and then the
heating head/work coil before returning to the chiller.
The vacuum and gas subsystem starts from the 35\,mm-ID fused-quartz tube that
forms the chamber, connected through a KF40 flange and flexible bellows to a
compact turbo pumping station
($\sim$1$\times$10$^{-6}$--10$^{-8}$\,Torr); a manual vent valve fed from an
inert-gas regulator allows controlled venting and
backfilling~\cite{kanda2007developmentofhightemperature,inaba1986effectsofthe}.
The atmosphere matters for graphite-crucible anneals: the best results were
achieved by pumping to high vacuum and then backfilling with argon through the
vent valve, purging before settling to a steady trickle ($\sim$20\,SCCM),
whereas heating under vacuum alone tended to sublime graphite onto the chamber
walls. For the highest-temperature ceramic work (Sec.~\ref{sec:ysz}), the
chamber was evacuated and then continuously filled with argon while the
turbopump was running, avoiding oxidation during heating.

Temperature is sensed optically: a dual-wavelength (ratio)
pyrometer~\cite{tapetado2016twocolorpyrometerfor,belikov2024fastmultiwavelengthpyrometer}
(800--2500\,\celsius{} sensing range) views the sample through a KF40
optical window, providing a non-contact signal robust to emissivity changes
and to the RF field that would corrupt
thermocouples~\cite{ham2016insituspectralemissivity,suleiman2025improvingpyrometryof,usamentiaga2014infraredthermographyfor}.
Control is implemented in LabVIEW, with VIs for manual control, automated
ramping, PID tuning, and email alerts: the DAQ's 0--5\,V analog output drives
a voltage-to-current loop conditioner whose 4--20\,mA output lands on the
generator's power-setpoint input, and the pyrometer's analog output returns to a DAQ input, closing
the PID loop. Below the pyrometer's $\sim$700\,\celsius{} detection floor the
VI ramps open-loop; once the pyrometer returns a sustained valid reading,
control hands over to closed-loop PID on the optical
temperature~\cite{meng2023designofvacuum,rapoport2006optimalcontrolof}.
Mechanically, a bolted support stand carries the generator's heating head,
centering the work coil on the crucible position; the vacuum chamber itself
has no support stand --- the chamber stack is joined by KF40 flanges to the
pyrometer housing, suspended from the ceiling by cables, so the vertical stack
hangs from above.

Metal charges in the graphite crucible reach the Ni/Fe
grain-growth range ($\sim$1400--1500\,\celsius) with demonstrated soaks from
minutes to $\sim$40\,h, and the separate tantalum-susceptor ceramic
configuration extends the working range to $\sim$2000--2350\,\celsius. The
layer transfers
to any generator that exposes a monotonic analog power input, supports a
water-cooled head/coil of the documented geometry, delivers enough RF power
for the target charge, and allows electrical isolation of the DAQ/control
path. When porting, the command scaling, calibration, PID gains, alignment,
and interlock behavior are re-established.

\subsection{Graphite crucible / susceptor}

Metal specimens are held in a two-piece graphite crucible machined in-house
from 3000\,\celsius-grade graphite stock: a cup body and a lidded cap with
a central bore that contains the charge while leaving an optical path for the
pyrometer. The graphite couples strongly to the induction field and acts as a
susceptor, heating the contained metal specimen by conduction and radiation.
The
specimen is sandwiched between two alumina discs in the cup, the lid seats on
top, and a sapphire window closes the lid bore
(Fig.~\ref{fig:crucible-assembly}); fully assembled the stack stands 13\,mm
tall, and the measured dimensions of every piece are given in the
supplementary material. Inside the vacuum chamber the crucible rests on an
alumina support rod that positions it at coil height, and crucibles are
repaired with graphite cement rather than discarded. The graphite crucible is
used exclusively for metal grain growth; ceramic charges are never loaded into
it, and instead use the entirely separate tantalum-susceptor stack of
Sec.~\ref{sec:ysz}.

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.82\linewidth]{fig_crucible_assembly.png}
\caption{Specimen-loading sequence for the graphite crucible/susceptor.
(a)~The specimen is placed between two alumina sheets in the crucible body.
(b)~The graphite lid is seated on top. (c)~The sapphire window closes the lid
bore, leaving an optical path for the pyrometer.}
\label{fig:crucible-assembly}
\end{figure}

%==============================================================
\section{Construction and operation}
%==============================================================

Following the heating path, the generator's heating head mounts on an
adjustable platform on the bolted support stand so the coil centers on the
crucible position of the vertically suspended quartz tube. The vacuum path
begins at the quartz tube, whose open end terminates in a KF40 flange closed
by a 55\,mm quartz optical disc. Every flanged joint compresses an elastomer
O-ring carried on a centering ring, so the stack assembles with hand-tightened
clamps and no tools, and these seals support the
$\sim$10$^{-6}$--10$^{-8}$\,Torr operating vacuum. From the bottom of the
stack, a tee carries a 0.5\,psi overpressure relief valve protecting the
quartz tube during backfilling, and a flexible bellows continues through the
wide-range vacuum gauge to the turbo pumping station. The roughing pump sits
on a separate support, mechanically decoupled so its vibration does not reach
the chamber or the pyrometer sight line. On the gas path, the argon regulator
feeds the manual vent valve; a mass flow controller between them is helpful
when continuous inert gas flow is required. On the control path, electrical
isolation is maintained between the
DAQ/control wiring and the generator interface, and manual control is verified
before automated ramp/soak is enabled.

A run begins with the chamber vented (never while the
turbo pump is spinning): the specimen is loaded into the crucible
(Fig.~\ref{fig:crucible-assembly}), the stack inserted, and the chamber pumped
down. The LabVIEW profile ramps open-loop below the pyrometer floor, hands
over to closed-loop PID, and runs the programmed soak; afterwards the sample
cools under vacuum and the chamber is vented only once the turbo pump has
stopped.

%==============================================================
\section{Performance validation}
%==============================================================

The furnace has completed more than one hundred logged anneals of Ni200 and
Ni4N5 nickel, YSZ, and Pd thermal-evaporation charges at
$\sim$900--1400\,\celsius, with soaks from minutes to $\sim$40\,h. The
supplementary material links each result below to its underlying run log.

For one fixed Ni4N5/quartz/graphite/optical configuration, four
steady-state anneals spanning 1200--1400\,\celsius{} give a monotonic relation
between soak-mean analog power command and soak-mean pyrometer temperature,
well described by $T \approx 931 + 632\,I$ (\celsius, $I$ in mA),
$R^2 = 0.991$. The offset shifts with charge and
optical configuration, so this is an operational calibration for a given
setup, not a universal command--temperature law. A Ni4N5 anneal at a nominal
1300\,\celsius{} / 12\,h condition serves as the representative closed-loop
run (trace in the supplementary material): the soak held
$1302.1 \pm 3.0$\,\celsius{} over the 12\,h plateau, with a 10--90\,\% rise
time of $\sim$1135\,s, overshoot of $\sim$12\,\celsius, and near-zero drift
($-0.02$\,\celsius/h).

Eight Ni4N5 anneals at a nominal 1200\,\celsius{} / 12\,h condition, run
under the same gas-flow configuration, reproduced their soak temperature to
$1201.2 \pm 1.3$\,\celsius{} (coefficient of variation
0.11\,\%). Long-duration stability was
demonstrated with two Ni200 anneals at 1325\,\celsius, held for 20\,h
($1326.5 \pm 1.5$\,\celsius, drift $-0.004$\,\celsius/h) and 40\,h (figures in
the supplementary material). Reported temperatures are closed-loop
ratio-pyrometer readings in a fixed optical geometry; absolute specimen
temperature still depends on emissivity and surface
condition~\cite{ham2016insituspectralemissivity,giulietti2025spectralemissivitymeasurement,kieruj2016determinationofemissivity}.

%==============================================================
\section{Microstructural validation}
%==============================================================

The furnace exists to change microstructure, so the decisive test is what the
anneals do to real specimens. Figure~\ref{fig:kikuchi} shows a raw
electron-backscatter (Kikuchi) pattern from a furnace-annealed nickel
specimen, reproduced exactly as the EBSD detector saved it during orientation
mapping. Even at the coarse $8\times8$ binning used for mapping, the bands
and zone axes are crisply resolved, and inverse-pole-figure maps built from
such patterns resolve hundreds of indexed grains per
specimen. Thermal grooving is itself a record of the anneal: edge-on views
of a specimen annealed at 1200\,\celsius{} / 12\,h, cleaned only
ultrasonically, show deep grooves and boundaries traversing the full sheet
thickness, a step toward columnar grain
structures~\cite{zhang2007dynamicsandmechanism}.

\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.36\linewidth]{fig_kikuchi_raw.jpg}
\caption{Raw electron-backscatter (Kikuchi) pattern from a furnace-annealed

mapping (the detector's $8\times8$-binned output). The crisply resolved bands
and zone axes index directly to the nickel lattice.}
\label{fig:kikuchi}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:microstructure} shows the microstructure such an anneal

\subsection{Extension to ceramic charges: YSZ}
\label{sec:ysz}

Grain growth in refractory ceramics ideally follows an Arrhenius behavior
that is sensitive to both time and temperature, so the higher soak
temperatures this furnace reaches translate directly into far shorter times
to equivalent grain growth. The same control, vacuum, and sensing stack
extends to charges that do not couple to the RF field at all, demonstrated
with grain growth in 8\,mol\% yttria-stabilized zirconia (YSZ). Ceramic grain
growth requires soak temperatures beyond the graphite/alumina stack's service
range, so only the charge stack is exchanged: the YSZ specimen is sandwiched
between two 25.5\,mm tantalum susceptor blocks seated on top of a boron
nitride ceramic stand which sits on an alumina support rod. The generator,
vacuum path, pyrometer, and control are untouched (schematic and micrographs
in the supplementary material). With two tantalum blocks a temperature of
2500\,\celsius{} was reliably reached. The practical temperature ceiling so
far is overheating of the induction coils and susceptor failure due to
chemical compatibility, not available RF power.

At these temperatures, chemical compatibility between specimen, susceptor,
and crucible becomes the design constraint: a Ta block atop the YSZ
at 2500\,\celsius{}/3.5\,h
grew grains successfully to 200\,\textmu m but contaminated the sample with
Ta; YSZ set into a graphite
susceptor at 2000\,\celsius{}/168\,h was consumed by the carbon; and a
Ta/YSZ/Ta sandwich at 2130\,\celsius{}/80.4\,h formed a Ta--BN reaction shell.
Figure~\ref{fig:graingrowth} shows the outcome of the first fully successful
ceramic anneal, 45\,min at 2500\,\celsius: the grain-boundary network of the
YSZ surface after coarsening from 20 to 90\,\textmu m, growth
that a conventional 1600\,\celsius{} box furnace pushed to its limits
needed more than 228\,h to approach (10 to 80\,\textmu m).

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.66\linewidth]{fig_grain_growth.png}
\caption{As-recorded optical micrograph of the YSZ specimen after the
2500\,\celsius{} / 45\,min tantalum-susceptor anneal, which coarsened the
grains from $\sim$20 to $\sim$90\,\textmu m. The red multi-grain measurement
traces and the 100\,\textmu m scale bar are part of the original microscope
record.}
\label{fig:graingrowth}
\end{figure}

%==============================================================
\section{Conclusions}
%==============================================================

A bare commercial RF induction generator can be converted into a
computer-controlled, vacuum-integrated, pyrometer-feedback annealing system
with a documented, generator-agnostic modernization layer. On a current
solid-state generator the stack demonstrated a power--temperature
calibration with $R^2 = 0.991$, high soak repeatability,
stable closed-loop soaks up to 40\,h, and annealed nickel whose surfaces
index directly in the EBSD and show controlled grain growth.

The tantalum-susceptor variant shows how far the same furnace stretches
without redesign: exchanging only the charge stack carries the system from
metal anneals in the graphite crucible to ceramic grain growth beyond
2300\,\celsius, and the survey behind that result identified chemistry, not
power, as the governing constraint at extreme temperature. Every failure
mode was a contact reaction between charge, susceptor, and crucible, which
the interchangeable stack lets a user iterate run by run. Because the
modernization layer requires only a monotonic analog power-control input,
the design ports across generators and is directly reusable at a small
fraction of turn-key cost, and the through-thickness boundary grooving
points to columnar grain-growth studies as a natural next application.
