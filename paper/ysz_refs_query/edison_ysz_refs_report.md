## Recommended citations and manuscript wording

### 1. Arrhenius grain-growth kinetics of YSZ

**Recommended sentence**

> “Grain growth in yttria-stabilized zirconia is thermally activated: the rate constant in the grain-growth law follows an Arrhenius temperature dependence, making grain coarsening highly sensitive to annealing temperature.”

This formulation is more precise than saying merely that grain growth “follows Arrhenius kinetics,” because it is normally the kinetic coefficient in a relation such as \(G^n-G_0^n=K_0t\exp(-Q/RT)\) that has Arrhenius dependence; the growth exponent \(n\) depends on material and mechanism.

#### Best primary citation

1. **S. Tekeli and U. Demir, “Colloidal processing, sintering and static grain growth behaviour of alumina-doped cubic zirconia,” *Ceramics International* 31, 973–980 (2005). DOI: [10.1016/j.ceramint.2004.10.011](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ceramint.2004.10.011).**

   **Why this is the best citation:** It directly measures static grain growth in 8 mol% yttria-stabilized cubic zirconia over 1400–1600 °C and reports, for undoped 8YSCZ, a grain-growth exponent of 2 and an activation energy of 289 kJ mol⁻¹; alumina additions changed the exponent to 3 and the activation energy to 410 kJ mol⁻¹. This is unusually close to both your composition and your 1600 °C conventional-furnace comparison. (tekeli2005colloidalprocessingsintering pages 1-2)

#### Strong corroborating citation

2. **K. Matsui, H. Yoshida, and Y. Ikuhara, “Phase-transformation and grain-growth kinetics in yttria-stabilized tetragonal zirconia polycrystal doped with a small amount of alumina,” *Journal of the European Ceramic Society* 30, 1679–1690 (2010). DOI: [10.1016/j.jeurceramsoc.2010.01.007](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jeurceramsoc.2010.01.007).**

   **Why it is useful:** This independent YSZ study resolves thermally activated grain-growth contributions and reports activation energies of 588 and 640 kJ mol⁻¹ for the grain-boundary-diffusion and solute-drag-related terms, respectively, reinforcing the strong temperature sensitivity while illustrating that the fitted activation energy is composition- and mechanism-dependent. (matsui2010phasetransformationandgraingrowth pages 11-12)

**Citation recommendation:** Tekeli and Demir alone is sufficient immediately after the sentence; add Matsui *et al.* if you want to show that substantial activation barriers are found more broadly across YSZ compositions. Your 228 h versus 45 min observation should remain presented as your own furnace result, not as something predicted quantitatively by these lower-temperature kinetic fits—extrapolation from 1400–1600 °C to 2500 °C could be affected by mechanism changes, volatilization, porosity, or phase/composition evolution.

### 2. Graphite–alumina contact: the “1800 °C eutectic” claim

## Assessment

The sentence as written is **not adequately supported**. Alumina and graphite should not be described as a simple binary pair that “undergoes a eutectic reaction” at approximately 1800 °C. Carbon is a reactant, and the relevant high-temperature chemistry belongs to the multicomponent Al–O–C system: carbothermal reduction produces CO and may produce Al-bearing vapor, Al₄C₃, and aluminum oxycarbides such as Al₄O₄C. Phase relations in the reacted Al₂O₃–Al₄C₃ subsystem therefore do not establish a pristine Al₂O₃–C eutectic at 1800 °C.

The literature recovered here also does **not** justify a general statement that carbothermal reduction “begins at 1700–1900 °C” without specifying pressure, carbon activity, gas removal, dwell time, and detection criterion. A review of bulk alumina carbothermal processing instead places conventional high-conversion operation around 2100–2300 °C, while vacuum shifts equilibria and promotes volatile products and intermediate carbide/oxycarbide formation. (fernandezgonzalez2018concentratedsolarenergy pages 3-4)

**Recommended replacement sentence**

> “Direct contact between graphite and alumina was eliminated because, at very high temperature and particularly under vacuum or low oxygen potential, carbon can carbothermally reduce Al₂O₃, evolving CO and forming Al-bearing vapor species, Al₄C₃, and aluminum oxycarbides that degrade the interface.”

If brevity is essential:

> “Direct graphite–alumina contact was avoided because carbothermal reduction of Al₂O₃ under high-temperature vacuum conditions can form CO, volatile Al-bearing species, carbides, and oxycarbides, degrading the interface.”

Do **not** retain “approximately 1800 °C” unless you can identify a specific invariant reaction in an assessed Al₂O₃–Al₄C₃–Al₄O₄C phase diagram and state its composition and atmosphere; it should not be presented as an Al₂O₃–graphite eutectic.

#### Best primary references

1. **M. S. Walker and R. J. Fruehan, “Experimental verification of the thermodynamics of critical aspects of the carbothermic reduction of alumina. Part 1: Experimental result of reacting Al₂O₃–C mixtures,” *Metallurgical and Materials Transactions B* 44, 853–861 (2013). DOI: [10.1007/s11663-013-9839-3](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11663-013-9839-3).**

   **Why it is the strongest citation:** It is a direct experimental study of reacting Al₂O₃–C mixtures designed specifically to test the thermodynamics and products of alumina carbothermal reduction, making it the most direct reference for replacing the eutectic explanation with reactive-interface chemistry.

2. **M. Kruesi, M. E. Galvez, M. Halmann, and A. Steinfeld, “Solar aluminum production by vacuum carbothermal reduction of alumina—Thermodynamic and experimental analyses,” *Metallurgical and Materials Transactions B* 42, 254–260 (2011). DOI: [10.1007/s11663-010-9461-6](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11663-010-9461-6).**

   **Why it is appropriate:** It directly addresses alumina–carbon reactions under vacuum, the condition most relevant to your furnace, and treats the pressure-dependent formation and removal of aluminum-bearing gaseous products rather than invoking simple eutectic melting.

3. **J. H. Cox and L. M. Pidgeon, “An investigation of the aluminum–oxygen–carbon system,” *Canadian Journal of Chemistry* 41, 671–683 (1963). DOI: [10.1139/v63-095](https://doi.org/10.1139/v63-095).**

   **Why it is appropriate:** This classic experimental treatment of the Al–O–C system establishes that graphite–alumina compatibility must be understood through reduction, carbide/oxycarbide, CO, and vapor equilibria rather than as an inert binary oxide–carbon eutectic.

#### Optional specialist reference for the phase-diagram issue

**J.-M. Lihrmann, T. Zambetakis, and M. Daire, “High-temperature behavior of the aluminum oxycarbide Al₂OC in the system Al₂O₃–Al₄C₃ and with additions of aluminum nitride,” *Journal of the American Ceramic Society* 72, 1704–1709 (1989). DOI: [10.1111/j.1151-2916.1989.tb06306.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1151-2916.1989.tb06306.x).**

**Why it is useful:** It directly addresses high-temperature phase behavior in the reacted Al₂O₃–Al₄C₃/oxycarbide subsystem and is therefore the right citation when explaining why a nominal “Al₂O₃–C eutectic” description omits chemically essential intermediate phases.

For broader temperature context, Fernández-González *et al.* review carbothermal alumina processing at approximately 2100–2300 °C and report Al₄C₃, Al₄O₄C, and volatile Al₂O among vacuum-process products: **D. Fernández-González *et al.*, “Concentrated solar energy applications in materials science and metallurgy,” *Solar Energy* 170, 520–540 (2018), DOI: [10.1016/j.solener.2018.05.065](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.solener.2018.05.065).** (fernandezgonzalez2018concentratedsolarenergy pages 3-4)
